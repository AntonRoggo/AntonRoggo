## Phase 3: Execution

### HOOKS

My husband said I was "getting a little soft" at 63. Then he showed up uninvited to my girls trip in Tulum. He lasted three days.

### HOLD

Mark and I have been married for 26 years.

We met when I was 36. He was 40. Both working in tech in Dallas.

We got married a year later. Had two kids. Emma and Jake. Both grown now. Emma's in Seattle, Jake's in Denver.

For the first nineteen years, things were good.

We traveled. We hiked. We went to concerts.

Then Mark retired early. Two years ago. Sold his company. Made enough that we're comfortable.

That's when things changed.

He started playing golf five days a week. Long lunches with the same four men. His whole life became tee times and the clubhouse.

Most of the men he plays with are on their second wives. Younger. A lot younger.

And Mark started noticing things out loud.

Never about me. Just... out loud.

"Dave's wife walks past and every head at the table turns. Woman's got a waist like a college kid."

He'd say it in the car on the way home. Then he'd talk about the traffic.

I told myself he was just making conversation.

Then Thanksgiving.

Ray hugged me at the door and I felt him keep it short. Half a hug. Arms not quite meeting.

Ray has been Mark's closest friend since before I met either of them. He comes every year.

Later he was in the kitchen with Mark a long while. The water running the whole time.

That night, in our bedroom, Mark said: "I asked Ray about it today."

"About what?"

"He said he'd noticed it too. That you look different than you used to. Heavier, maybe. Puffy."

He said it gently. Like he was handing me something helpful.

"It's not a big deal."

It's not a big deal.

Twenty-six years and he never once said a word about it to me.

One afternoon in my own kitchen, and by ten o'clock he had.

A few weeks later, at the neighbors' barbecue, I sat down next to him on the wicker couch.

After a minute he stood up. "Going to get another beer."

He came back. He didn't sit down again.

By January he was stopping in the doorway of our bedroom.

Not crossing in. Talking to me from the threshold.

"Becca, you've gotten a little... soft. I don't know how else to put it. Maybe look into something. For yourself."

For yourself.

He smiled like it was a joke. Like he was being thoughtful.

I didn't laugh.

That night I stood in the bathroom and looked at myself for the first time in two years.

I'm 63.

Twenty-two pounds since Mark retired. My belly never went down. My face puffy every morning. My jawline gone. The woman in the mirror looked like she'd been carrying something heavy up a flight of stairs for two years and never set it down.

I'd walked every morning for a year. Cut wine. Cut bread. Did the app Emma set up for me. My doctor said at 63 this is what happens. Walk more. I was already walking.

Nothing moved.

In February I got a text from four women I went to the University of Texas with. We'd stayed close for over thirty years.

"Girls trip. Tulum. Five days. End of March. Who's in?"

I said yes before I put the phone down.

When I told Mark over dinner, he put down his fork.

"Five days? In Mexico?"

"Yes."

"Becca, you sure that's a good idea?"

"Why wouldn't it be?"

He looked at me carefully. "Just... five days in close quarters with four other women. Heat. Sun all day. Swimsuits. I'm just thinking about you."

I'm just thinking about you.

"I'm going."

"Of course. Just pack accordingly. Bring the cover-ups. Maybe more of them."

I left the table before I said something I'd regret.

The next morning I drove over to Carol's. Seven weeks before the flight.

Carol's sixty-nine. Twenty-eight years on a geriatric ward before she retired. She lives ten minutes away and has never once pretended not to notice something.

I told her about the doorway. About the wicker couch. About getting a little soft.

She let me finish. Then she poured the coffee.

She put something on the table between us. Small. Dark glass. A dropper lid. The liquid inside looked like honey.

She didn't say a word about it.

"He's right that there's something," she said. "He's a liar about what it is. Those two aren't the same."

"I walk every day."

"I know you do."

"Then what?"

"Twenty-eight years I looked after women our age and older. Things change. Your hair goes, your eyes go, your skin gets drier."

"Nobody's fault. You color it, you get glasses."

"Well. Around sixty it also stops draining."

"Stops what?"

"Your lymphatic system. It's the body's drain. Everything your body makes and doesn't need goes out through it. It has no pump, so it slows down on its own around our age. And then two years of a man noticing things out loud in the car does the rest. Cortisol shuts it the rest of the way."

"I've never heard of that."

"Nobody has. People don't talk about it."

"Then I'll walk twice a day."

"That's the part everybody gets wrong."

She set her cup down.

"Ordinary dieting doesn't take it off."

"Now you're being ridiculous."

"Think about a sink that drains slow. You run less water. And?"

I didn't answer her.

"And?"

"It still fills."

"It still fills. You put less in and it stayed anyway."

"Because the problem was never how much you put in. It's that nothing's going out."

"What our bodies stop doing is called draining. Works exactly the same way. The waste sits there. Thickens. Hardens into sludge. In your belly. In your face. Under your jaw. That's not fat, Becca. That's waste. You can walk for the rest of your life. You'll never burn it off. You can't burn waste. You have to drain it."

I sat there with that.

"Then what gets rid of it?"

She pushed the bottle across the table.

"I was hoping you'd ask me before I had to offer."

"Four herbs. Stillingia breaks down the years of hardened waste. Prickly ash restarts the circulation the stress shut down. Cleavers sweeps all of it out. Red clover calms the swelling so it doesn't build back up. Half a dropper under your tongue, morning and night. Tastes like honey."

"And it works?"

"I put half my ward on it. I've used it for years."

"One morning it occurred to me I'd stopped bracing every time somebody hugged me. I never knew I'd been doing it."

"It's called Lymphoria. Order more while you can. They make it in small batches and it sells out. Six weeks. You've got seven."

I drove home with it in my purse.

I used it that night. Half a dropper. It's drops. I didn't expect anything.

Week one. Bathroom every hour. Day four, my face less puffy in the morning. Day six, my wedding ring spinning loose on my finger.

Week two. Belt one notch tighter. I woke up one morning and felt lighter. Like something had been pressing on my chest and stopped.

Week three. Jawline back. Sleeping through the night for the first time since Mark retired. Emma FaceTimed about Easter and stopped mid-sentence. "Mom. What did you do? You look different."

"Nothing."

"That's not nothing."

Week four. Down fourteen pounds. Eating normally. Mark said nothing. Mark had stopped looking at me two years ago, and he wasn't going to start in the kitchen.

Week five. Down nineteen pounds. The swimsuit I had almost talked myself out of buying. The one I'd bought a size up and hidden in the drawer. Fit. I stood in the bathroom in it and didn't reach for a cover-up.

Week six. I packed.

Mark came into the bedroom. Stopped at the threshold like always.

"That's all you're bringing?"

"That's all I need."

"You sure? You should bring extra stuff. For the heat. You know how you get."

I zipped the bag.

"I'm sure."

The morning of the flight I called an Uber. Mark was making coffee.

"You sure you don't want me to drive you?"

"I'm fine."

"Alright. Have fun. Try not to overdo it."

I left.

The Uber dropped me at the terminal.

I walked in.

And I saw him.

Mark.

Standing at the check-in counter. With two suitcases.

He turned and saw me.

"Surprise," he said.

I just stared.

"What are you doing here?"

"I couldn't stop thinking about it. You going all that way alone. I got worried."

"Mark. I'm going with my friends."

"I know. But still. I just want to make sure you're okay. I booked the same flight. I'll stay at a different resort. Do my own thing. Just be nearby in case."

He said it like he was doing me a favor.

"You booked the same flight without telling me."

"I wanted it to be a surprise."

The agent behind the counter was watching us.

Both his bags came out overweight. $75 each. He paid $150 in fees while I walked to the next counter.

"Just the one bag?"

"Just the one."

At security, Mark got pulled aside. Shoes. Belt. Watch.

I went through and walked to the gate.

We landed in Tulum. I met my friends at the rideshare pickup.

"Is he really here?"

"He's really here."

"What is he thinking?"

"He thinks I need supervision."

I checked into the resort. Unpacked in five minutes. Changed into the swimsuit.

When I came out into the suite my college roommate Maya was sitting on the bed.

She tilted her head slightly.

"Becca. You look incredible. What did you do?"

"Drops."

"From where?"

"A friend gave them to me."

She kept her eyes on me a second too long. Then she smiled. "Send me the link when you get home."

I met my friends at the pool.

An hour later, Mark showed up. At our resort.

Sunburned already. Forgot sunscreen. Same shirt from the plane.

"There you are," he said. "I checked into my place down the road. Just wanted to make sure you got here okay."

My friends stared.

"I'm fine, Mark."

He stood there. Awkward.

That night my friends and I went to dinner. Mark texted: "Where are you guys eating?" I didn't answer it.

Day two. Mark at our pool again. Still sunburned. Wearing a gift-shop T-shirt that said "Tulum."

"One of my bags is still missing."

We went out snorkeling. Mark didn't come. "Too tired."

Day three. Lunch at the beachside restaurant.

A woman in her thirties went past our table. Stopped. Came back.

"I'm sorry," she said. "You look incredible. Can I ask what you're doing?"

She laughed at herself.

"Sorry. I've interrupted your lunch. It just stood out to me."

"It's drops."

"What's it called?"

"Lymphoria."

She typed it into her phone. "I'm going to find this. Thank you."

She walked away.

Nobody at our table said anything for a moment.

Then Maya: "Becca. That happened yesterday too."

"What?"

"At the front desk. The girl checking us in. She looked at you twice while you were talking to her. I noticed. I just didn't say anything."

Mark was at the next table over. He'd watched the woman come over. He'd heard all of it.

He looked at his lunch and didn't look up.

I sat there a moment. Watched him not looking up.

And I thought about Ray at Thanksgiving. About the hug at the door and his arms not meeting.

About my husband walking into the kitchen to ask his oldest friend whether his wife had gotten heavy.

Neither of them ever said it to me. They said it to each other.

I think I understood now.

Day four I posted photos from the snorkeling trip.

Me and my friends. Tan. Smiling. Looking healthy.

Within an hour the comments came in.

My sister: "You look AMAZING."

Emma: "Mom!! This is so cool!"

Then Mark's phone started buzzing. He was at the pool near us.

I could see his face changing as he read.

Dave: "Bro, Becca looks incredible. What are you doing at home?"

Tom: "Your wife's living her best life."

Steve: "Dude. When did Becca get so fresh?"

Mark put the phone down. Looked at me.

"You were right," he said quietly.

"About what?"

"About this trip. About me being wrong."

My friends were pretending not to listen.

"I think I should head home," he said. "Let you finish this with your friends. I'm sorry."

He left that afternoon.

We spent the last two days on the beach. No interruptions. Nobody hovering.

When I got home he was sitting in the living room with the lamp on.

"I don't want us to keep going the way we've been going," he said.

"Neither do I."

"I watched you walk into that lobby in Tulum," he said. "And you looked like you'd outgrown me. That's what scared me. So I made you smaller instead of getting bigger."

"You did."

"I know. I don't want to be that."

We talked until two in the morning. Actually talked, which we hadn't done in about three years.

Carol called last week to ask about the trip.

I told her all of it. The airport. The woman at the restaurant. What Mark did when I got home.

She laughed for a long time.

Then: "They opened a new batch. It's already most of the way gone. Once it's out you're waiting."
