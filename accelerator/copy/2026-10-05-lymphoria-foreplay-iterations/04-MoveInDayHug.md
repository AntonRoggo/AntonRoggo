## HOLD

My son left home for college last weekend.

Let me tell you what that does to a mother at sixty.

You give eighteen years to raising one person. You watch him turn into himself. You pack the lunches, you scrub grass stains out of the soccer jersey, you nag him about his vitamins.

Then one August Saturday you cram four boxes, a duffel and a desk fan in the back of the SUV, drive him three states over, help him wrestle sheets onto a twin mattress in a room that smells like bleach, hug him beside the car, and make the trip home by yourself.

None of those years come back to you.

I'm sixty.

His name is Caleb. He's eighteen.

For two years, Caleb had barely looked at me.

He wasn't cruel about it.

He just quit.

He needed me. He leaned on me. Once in a while he said thanks.

But I wasn't a person to him anymore.

I was a service. Rides, laundry, dinner.

And I'd quit hoping for anything else.

Three weeks before move-in, Margaret came by for coffee.

We've been friends twenty-two years. She's fifty-eight.

She recruits executives for a living. Chief financial officers, company presidents, people who are used to talking first. For thirty years she has walked into their boardrooms and made them wait on her.

She put her mug down on my counter and studied me.

"Gwen. In three weeks you'll drop Caleb at school and drive back to an empty house. And you've spent two years hiding in his old hoodies like you're apologizing for being in the room."

I didn't say anything.

She waited.

"I've watched it happen. Your face is puffy every single morning. Your jawline's gone. You've gone up two sizes and you live in elastic waistbands. You duck out of every photo. And don't tell me you stopped trying, because I've watched you try everything. Something in your body stopped working, and nobody has told you what. You're my friend, so I'm telling you."

She stopped.

"That parking lot is the picture Caleb is going to keep. You belong in it as yourself. Not hiding behind a sweatshirt."

I didn't answer.

She got up, went to her purse, and came back with a small dark dropper bottle. She set it between our mugs.

"This is Lymphoria. I've been on it four months."

I'd already noticed something had happened to her. Margaret had always carried a little extra through the middle, same as me. Since spring she'd gotten smaller. Her face was sharper. At her firm's summer party she wore a belted dress, and I'd assumed she'd hired a trainer.

"My niece Dana is a nurse," she said. "Eleven years at a lymphatic clinic in Portland. She watched me go up a size every year after fifty and finally sat me down at Easter."

"I don't need another diet, Margaret," I said.

"Sweetheart, that's exactly the mistake. This isn't a diet. Nobody is telling you to eat less."

"Then what is it?"

She took a breath. "Dana made me learn this word for word, so listen."

"You don't have menopause weight. You have menopause sludge."

I listened.

"When estrogen drops your lymphatic system stops working. Every glass of wine. Every hormone. Every toxin from the last thirty years that your body was supposed to drain. Just sits there. Locked in. Hardened. Stuck."

"That's not fat. That's waste. You can diet for the rest of your life. You'll never burn it off. Because you can't burn waste. You have to drain it."

I couldn't say a word.

"Every plan you've tried, the shakes, the walking, the counting, goes after fat. Not one of them touches the drain. So you starve yourself, lose three pounds, and your belly doesn't budge."

I nodded. My whole last decade, in one sentence.

She picked the bottle back up.

"Four herbs. Stillingia breaks down the years of hardened waste. Prickly ash restarts the circulation menopause shut down. Cleavers sweeps the loosened waste out. Red clover calms the swelling so it doesn't build back up."

"Half a dropper under the tongue, morning and night. Tastes like honey."

"So you're not hiding anything under a bigger shirt. The stuff that was stuck actually leaves. That's why it doesn't come back."

She pushed it across the counter.

"They make it in small batches and it keeps selling out, so I ordered yours when I reordered mine. Take it morning and night for three weeks. When you walk Caleb up those dorm stairs, you'll have had twenty-one mornings of being a woman who's about to be noticed."

That night I went and stood in front of the bathroom mirror.

For two years I hadn't looked at my own stomach in that mirror without turning sideways and then turning away.

In the closet behind me sat a scale I'd stopped stepping on, a crate of meal-replacement shakes, and two fitness trackers with dead batteries.

A little museum of everything I'd tried since fifty-two.

I unscrewed the bottle.

Half a dropper under my tongue.

It really did taste like honey. Warm, a little earthy.

Nothing like the chalky shakes. Nothing like the fat burner pills that made my heart race.

Gone in a few seconds.

Nothing happened.

Not that night.

But it was the first night in two years I let myself look a little longer.

Morning and night, for three weeks. Even the days I stayed home in my robe.

Week one, I was in the bathroom every hour. By day four, my face was less puffy when I woke up.

Week two, my belt went in a notch. The bloat that had lived in my belly since fifty-two was finally going down.

By week three my jawline was back, and I'd started catching my reflection in the oven door on purpose. The scale I'd been avoiding said nine pounds.

One morning that week my husband James came into the kitchen for coffee and stopped short. "Gwen. Your face. You look like you again. What did you change?"

It had been close to five years since he'd said anything like that.

I thanked him.

I kept the rest to myself.

Move-in day fell on a Saturday.

We were backing out of the driveway by five-thirty. Nine hours. Three state lines.

Caleb rode shotgun, earbuds in, not a word for three hours.

I had on dark jeans I hadn't been able to button in four years and a soft cream blouse, tucked in. Half a dropper before we left.

Hour four, the earbuds came out.

"Mom. I'm not sure I can do this."

"You can."

"What if I hate it there?"

"Then we figure it out together."

He went quiet.

Then: "Mom, you've been looking different."

"How so?"

He glanced across at me. "I don't know. Lighter. Younger. You look good."

The earbuds went back in.

I drove the last stretch one hand steering, the other wrapped around my coffee, and for the first time in years I felt like my son could actually see me.

We reached campus around three.

The parking lot was a madhouse. Hundreds of families. Minivans and SUVs nosed in along the curb. Dollies, cardboard, mothers crying behind sunglasses, fathers hauling mini fridges.

We piled Caleb's things on the sidewalk. Four boxes, the duffel, the desk fan, a little lamp, and the basketball hoop he would not leave home without.

His room was three flights up, and the building had no elevator.

I took the duffel and a box up. Caleb did the rest in three trips.

On the third he came back with a tall, lanky kid in a basketball jersey.

"Mom, this is Tyson. My roommate."

Tyson offered his hand. "Hi, Mrs. Carter. Good to meet you."

I took it.

"Okay, sorry, this is weird to say," he said, "but you look amazing. Like one of the moms in my mom's magazines."

He laughed at himself. So did I.

Out of the corner of my eye, I saw Caleb taking it in.

Not long after, Tyson's mother came puffing up the stairwell. Janet. Mid-fifties. A box on her hip and a phone pinned between her ear and her shoulder.

She set the box on the desk and gave me a once-over.

"So you're Caleb's mother?"

"Guilty. Gwen."

"Janet. I belong to Tyson." We shook. Then she tilted her head. "Wait. You just did nine hours in a car and you look this put-together? Two hours in a car and my face looks like a balloon. I have been fighting this stomach since I turned fifty. What are you doing?"

Before I could answer, someone knocked on the open door.

Susan, the Resident Director. Early forties, in charge of the whole floor.

She shook Caleb's hand first.

He turned to her and said, "This is my mom. She's the one who drove me down."

The way he said it. I hadn't heard that voice from him since he was nine.

Proud. Precise. A boy introducing his mother to someone who counts.

Susan shook my hand. "Mrs. Carter, welcome. He's in good hands with us." She paused. "And can I just say, you look wonderful. Nine hours behind the wheel and you look this fresh? I'd love to know your secret."

At the far end of the hall, a nineteen-year-old was watching. He leaned toward his own mother, nodded in my direction, and said something.

She turned and stared at me, and kept staring.

I'll never know what he told her.

Tyson rolled his eyes at the whole scene. "Every mom does this."

Janet swatted his arm. "Give it twenty years."

All of us laughed.

By six the room was done. Bed made. Lamp plugged in. Hoop hanging over the closet door.

At about seven, Caleb walked me down to the car.

The sun was low. Other parents were making the same walk. Some were already crying.

We stopped at the back of the SUV.

"Mom."

"What is it, honey?"

A few seconds of nothing.

Then he opened his arms.

It had been six years since my son hugged me like that.

He didn't let go. Close to a full minute.

Two cars down, Tyson's parents saw the whole thing.

Into my shoulder he said, "Thanks for everything, Mom."

"I love you, baby."

"Love you too."

He stepped back and looked at me once more. Really looked.

"Drive safe. Text me when you're home."

"Promise."

Then he turned around and crossed the lot back to his dorm.

I sat in the driver's seat a good ten minutes before I could start the car.

It should have taken nine hours. It took me eight.

My headlights hit the garage door at three in the morning. My husband had waited up.

One look at my face and he asked, "How are you?"

"James. He hugged me. Almost a whole minute, right there by the car."

He wrapped me up. Then he leaned back and said, "Gwen. You look beautiful. Let's go to bed."

Three days after that, Caleb video called.

He was sitting cross-legged on his dorm bed.

"Hey Mom. Random question. What's that stuff you take called? Tyson's mom asked me three times on Saturday what you'd been doing. I kept saying no idea, that's just how she looks now. Now she's texting Tyson about it every day."

I spelled it for him.

He thumbed it into his phone. "Cool. I'll send it to her."

Then he hesitated.

"Mom. You looked really pretty Saturday. I meant to tell you. Sorry it took three days."

"It wasn't three days, honey. It was eighteen years. Every one of them worth it."

He grinned. "Love you, Mom."

"Love you more."

The screen went dark.

Here's what I took from all of it.

My own son spent two years barely looking at me.

Not because he'd stopped loving me.

Because menopause had locked so much into my body that the woman in his kitchen every morning, puffy, heavy through the middle, hiding in his old hoodies, didn't look like the mother he grew up with. None of that was my doing. My drain had shut down and nobody told me.

Margaret caught it before I ever did.

She set a little dark bottle by my coffee three weeks ahead of move-in and told me: morning and night.

She told me that parking lot was the picture Caleb would keep, and that I belonged in it as myself.

On move-in day, his roommate's mom couldn't stop asking about me.

The woman in charge of his floor called me wonderful.

Some kid down the hall nudged his mother and nodded my way.

And my son held onto me in that parking lot for a full minute.

With his roommate's parents watching.

Six years since he'd hugged me like that.

The drops didn't hand me a new body. They drained what menopause had locked into the one I already had. And every morning and every night for three weeks, I took them knowing my son would carry that dorm-room day with him for the rest of his life.
