## HOLD

From freshman year until the spring of junior year, Danielle Prescott and I were joined at the hip. We ate at the same lunch table. We slept over at each other's houses every Friday. In every group picture she stood dead center, and I stood a half step off to the side. Everybody agreed Danielle was the pretty one. Me, I got "the smart one."

If anyone ever called you "the smart one," you know what they meant by it. They meant you weren't the other thing. They meant they were being polite about how you looked.

Then senior year, Danielle got together with Michael. My Michael. I'd been going out with him for five months.

She didn't ask. She didn't say sorry. One Monday she just walked down the hallway with his arm draped over her, and gave me a look like I ought to get it. And I got it.

Boys didn't pick me. Boys made do with me until a Danielle came along.

That was the fall of 1985. I'm 58 now.

I went into accounting. Made partner. Bought a house on my own. After my divorce, I raised my daughter by myself. I'm proud of the life I put together, every brick of it.

But that old feeling, the "she's the pretty one, not you" feeling, never really went away. It just stopped talking.

Then, about two months ago, it started up again.

The reunion invitation had gone out that spring. Forty years. A rooftop bar downtown. Drinks, a hired photographer, a slideshow of our old yearbook pictures. I clicked yes without giving it a second thought.

Three days later Danielle put up her first countdown selfie. Full makeup. Ring light. A filter so thick she looked sanded. "Sixty days until reunion!! Who else is going??" She tagged me. I left it alone.

A week after that came another one. New selfie, same filter. Chin tilted just so, to show off the cheekbones a weight-loss shot had carved out for her. "Getting reunion ready! Any excuse for a glow-up, am I right ladies?"

Then she found my brunch picture. Me and my daughter on a Sunday morning. Just a regular photo. No filter on it.

Under it, for everyone to see, Danielle wrote: "Omg babe your face is SO puffy here! Ever tried cutting carbs?"

Forty-seven likes. Twelve replies. Every one of them from a woman we graduated with. I sat there staring at it far longer than I should have.

Then a private message came in. "Rach!! SO happy you're coming! Okay don't kill me, but have you considered shapewear for the night? The photographer's going to be shooting full length and I want you feeling your best! I can send you my whole routine. I've been doing fasting plus a body shaper for YEARS"

She wanted me to understand that she had a system. A method. Decades of practice at looking better than me.

"I'm good, thanks," I wrote back.

"Totally! But honestly if you change your mind, I have put SO much time into figuring out how to keep a bloated belly and a puffy face under control. It's basically a science lol. Just say the word!"

A science. She needed me to know it was a science.

Four days went by, and another message arrived. "Hiii just checking in because you've been on my mind. Have you looked into the shot? Because cutting carbs alone won't do it if your face is as puffy and your jawline's as gone as it looks in your pictures. You need the shot, THEN the shaper, THEN a black dress, or it all shows by the second drink. Anyway, only looking out for you!! See you soon!"

Looking out for me. Kindness, sharpened into a blade.

"See you there, Danielle."

"YAY!! It's going to be the best night!! Oh and Michael's coming btw, did you hear?"

Michael. Naturally. She had to make sure I knew. She wanted me picturing myself beside her, with him watching.

Four decades on, and she still couldn't help herself.

I set my phone down and called Gina. She's done my facials for six years, and she does lymphatic drainage massage at her studio, most of it on women our age. She'll tell you the truth whether you asked for it or not. I've seen her talk clients out of treatments they didn't need. She would rather lose a booking than sell you something that won't help.

Two weeks before, I'd left her a voicemail asking for the name of the med spa doctor her clients use for the weight-loss shot. She never called back. I figured she was slammed.

When I told her the whole thing, the comment, the messages, Michael, the rooftop, she was silent for a beat.

Then: "That voicemail you left me."

"Right. You never got back to me."

"I didn't get back to you deliberately."

Nobody spoke.

"Rachel, you don't need the shot. Sit down."

"I am sitting."

"Good. I'm about to save you a small fortune and six months of nausea."

She took a breath. "Let me tell you what's really going on. The belly that won't go down, the face that's puffy every morning, the jaw that's disappeared into your neck. It isn't because you need a needle that kills your appetite. Half my table work is lymphatic drainage, and I feel the same thing under my hands on every woman over fifty who comes in. You don't have menopause weight. You have menopause sludge. When estrogen drops your lymphatic system stops working. Every glass of wine. Every hormone. Every toxin from the last thirty years that your body was supposed to drain. Just sits there. Locked in. Hardened. Stuck. That's not fat. That's waste. You can diet for the rest of your life. You'll never burn it off. Because you can't burn waste. You have to drain it. That's why you look puffy even in a week you ate perfectly."

"So what do I do about it?"

"You drain it first. That's the part no diet, no shaper, and no shot ever touches. The shapewear squeezes it somewhere else for the night. The shot shrinks your appetite and hollows out your cheeks, and the waste stays right where it was."

"And something actually drains it?"

"One thing does." She texted me a link. "Lymphoria. Four herbs, liquid, under your tongue. Stillingia breaks down the years of hardened waste. Prickly ash restarts the circulation menopause shut down. Cleavers sweeps the loosened waste out. Red clover calms the swelling so it doesn't build back up."

"Drops."

"Drops. Half a dropper under the tongue, morning and night. Tastes like honey. That's the whole routine."

I didn't say anything for a second. "Gina. The reunion is six weeks from now."

"I know. That's exactly why I'm telling you today." She paused. "They make it in small batches, and when a batch sells out you wait weeks for the next one. There's a thirty-day money-back guarantee, so if you don't see anything, send it back. You'll see something."

"How can you be sure?"

"Because I've been taking it since spring. Last Thursday a woman on my table asked who did my jaw. I'm fifty-three. Nobody has ever asked me that in my life."

"And six weeks is enough time."

"Six weeks is plenty of time for Danielle to wish she'd never hit send."

I ordered before bed.

Three days later the bottle showed up. Small. Dark glass. Plain label.

I took my first half dropper that night. Under the tongue. Honey, just like she said. Ten seconds, start to finish.

The first week, I was up and down to the bathroom every hour. By day four, the face that met me in the mirror each morning was less puffy. Like something had finally let go.

By week two, my belt was a notch tighter. The bloat that had lived in my belly since menopause was going down. Nothing was holding it in. There was just less of it.

By week four, I ran my fingers along my jaw and found an edge there. A real one. Like something underneath had been dug back out. My rings turned on my fingers. The scale moved for the first time in three years.

By week five, I'd stopped checking my stomach sideways in the mirror. It was just my body. The one from fifteen years back.

On reunion night, I was dressed in twelve minutes. Shower, then moisturizer. Half a dropper. The black dress I'd been saving. Earrings. Lipstick. Mascara.

No shapewear. No cardigan to cover my middle. No tucking my chin for the camera.

I looked at myself and saw someone I hadn't seen in a long time. The girl I was before anyone decided I was "the smart one."

People were already crowding the rooftop when I got there. String lights overhead. A DJ spinning eighties songs. Faces I sort of knew.

I found Danielle right away. She was by the bar, one hand resting on Michael's arm.

She looked... assembled. Like a woman who'd done everything on the list in her bathroom and forgotten that a rooftop full of people isn't a selfie. Her cheeks were hollow from the shot, but her face was still puffy around the eyes. The shaper had rolled down and left a ridge under her dress. She kept tugging the hem and sucking in every time someone raised a phone. She looked like a woman holding her body in place all night.

I went past her toward the bar. At first she didn't know who I was. Then it landed.

"Rachel?" She actually put her drink down. "Oh my God. Rachel?"

"Hey, Danielle."

Her eyes went over my face. My jaw. My waist, specifically.

"You look..." Her eyes widened. "What happened to you?"

"Nothing. I just came."

"No. Your face. Where's the... how is your jaw like that? You always had..." She stopped herself. "I mean, you look fantastic. Who did your work?"

"No work."

"Oh, stop. The shot?"

"No shot."

She just stared. It's a specific kind of confusion, the face of someone who has spent forty years winning a game the other person never signed up for, at the exact moment she realizes the score has flipped.

Michael stepped up next to her.

"Rachel." He smiled. A real smile. "You look amazing. Let me get you a drink?"

He asked me. Not her. With her standing right there.

I'm not going to pretend that didn't hit precisely where it should have.

"Sure. A gin and tonic."

Danielle didn't say a word.

About forty minutes later I was at the bathroom mirror fixing my lipstick when the woman at the next sink turned to me. Janet Hollis. We'd sat together in AP English.

She was looking at me in the mirror. "Rachel. Okay. I have to say something."

"Go for it."

"I honestly assumed you'd had surgery. You look younger than you did senior year. Your jawline, your waist, your face is so rested. What happened to you?"

"Lymphatic drops."

"Drops?"

"Half a dropper, morning and night. Six weeks. It drained what menopause had locked in. That's why the puffiness is gone."

Janet slowly shook her head. "I spent twelve hundred dollars on fat freezing last month, and my belly looks exactly the same. What are they called?"

"Lymphoria."

"I'm ordering them right now." Her phone was out before I'd capped my lipstick.

Back out on the rooftop, the event photographer, a twenty-something with a big camera and a clipboard, tapped me on the shoulder.

"Hi. Excuse me. I'm so sorry to interrupt."

"You're fine."

"I've been taking pictures for hours, and I just have to ask. Could I use a few of yours for the reunion page? You photograph..." She hesitated. "Honestly? Beautifully. You could pass for fifteen years younger than anybody here. I won't even have to edit them."

Across the rooftop, I could see Danielle watching her talk to me.

"Please," I told her. "Shoot as many as you want."

It was close to eleven when Danielle tracked me down at the bar. She took the stool beside mine. Put her phone on the counter, screen down. Laced her fingers together.

She looked worn out. Not from the late hour. From the upkeep. From slipping into the bathroom every forty-five minutes to yank the shaper back up. From holding her breath in every photo.

Neither of us said anything for a minute.

"Okay," she finally said. "Spill it."

"Spill what?"

"What. You. Had. Done." One word at a time. "Because I'm looking at you. I'm looking at your jaw. There's nothing holding you in. No shaper. No shot. Nothing. And you look better than me, and I've got both." She breathed in. "I just booked lipo under my chin for next Tuesday. Four grand. And the woman I was doing it to beat? She's on the stool beside me in a black dress with nothing underneath it, and the photographer wants her for the reunion page."

I studied her. The girl who took my boyfriend. The girl who handed me "the smart one" like a consolation prize. The girl who told the whole class my face looked puffy. The girl who filled my inbox with diet advice like I was her charity case.

"It's called Lymphoria," I told her. "Four herbs. Half a dropper, morning and night. Six weeks. It doesn't hide anything. It drains what menopause locked in, so the bloat and the puffiness go away on their own."

She looked at me. "Drops."

"Drops. Ten seconds, twice a day. That's my entire routine."

"Six weeks."

"Six weeks. To the day."

She turned her phone over. "Where do I get them?"

"I'll text you the link. They make it in small batches, though. When it sells out, you're waiting weeks for the next one."

"And it really works."

I touched my jaw. "It works."

Then the farewell slideshow started. Our senior pictures filled a screen on the back wall. And there the two of us were. Danielle front and center, beaming, a homecoming crown on her head. And me, half a step to the side. Smaller. Quieter. The smart one.

Tonight the photographer had chosen my picture for the event page. Forty years on, I'd finally made it to the middle.

Here's what I've figured out. Danielle got forty years of being the pretty one. I got forty years of believing I wasn't. She kept it going every chance she had, in the hallway, in the comments, in my inbox six weeks out.

She came to our 40th in a shaper, with a hollowed-out face from the shot and four thousand dollars of lipo booked for Tuesday. I came in a black dress with nothing underneath and a little bottle I'd been taking for six weeks.

Lymphoria didn't turn me into someone pretty. It just drained away what was hiding the woman who always was.

The photographer put my face on the event page. A classmate assumed I'd had surgery. Michael offered ME the drink. And Danielle, who'd spent four decades making sure I knew I was the plain one, took the stool next to mine and asked what I was taking.

I wouldn't call that revenge. I'd call it the record, corrected.

Because walking into your high school reunion with a jawline you thought was gone for good and a face the photographer picks out of the crowd, while the girl who felt sorry for you is stuck at the bar in a rolled-down shaper with four thousand dollars of lipo she's suddenly second-guessing?
