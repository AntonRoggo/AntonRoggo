## HOLD

Nadia was turning thirty that October, and she'd been building the Barcelona trip for close to two years. Five of her friends, four nights, a boutique hotel tucked into the Gothic Quarter.

I was the first one she invited. I said yes before she finished the sentence.

Nobody mentioned David was on the list too. Or that he was bringing Simone.

I learned it fourteen months out, on a Tuesday, when Nadia phoned.

"Mom, there's something you should hear from me."

"Go ahead."

"Dad's coming. Simone too." She stopped. "And Simone wants to help plan. She said Dad told her the weight's been really hard on you since menopause. She just wants to pick stuff you'll be comfortable doing."

The weight.

Stuff I'd be comfortable doing.

I put my coffee down and didn't move.

For thirty years David watched me carry our whole life. I grew a three-person marketing team into a department of sixty. I raised two kids while his suitcase lived by the door. Every holiday, every funeral, every move had my handwriting on it.

Then menopause showed up when I was 50. My belly swelled out and never went back down. My face was puffy every single morning, and by noon it still hadn't settled. My jawline disappeared into my neck. I put on eighteen pounds around my middle eating the same food I'd always eaten. My good jeans went to the back of the closet. Then my wedding ring stopped going past the knuckle.

And David, who'd seen me hold up a household and a career at once, looked at me and saw someone who had to be planned around.

He moved out the next year. Simone had done the PR for a panel he spoke on. She was 38.

He gave her his edit of me. The woman who took the elevator. Who wore the long cardigan in August. Who sat out the walking part. The woman you made allowances for.

Now Simone was handing that woman to my daughter.

"I'm okay, Nadia," I said. "I'll book my own things."

Three days after that, a text from a number I didn't know.

Hi Jenny!! It's Simone, Nadia gave me your number, hope that's ok. Just want you to have mine for the trip. Barcelona gets SO hot in October and David said the heat's harder on you now with the extra weight. I'm here if you need anything 😊

The extra weight. In writing. About me. Sent to me.

All good, thanks, I wrote back.

Love that! Also FYI I'm only picking restaurants with booths instead of those tiny bistro chairs, so you'll be comfy at every dinner 😊

Comfy.

On Friday night she called.

"Hi! Quick question. Should I ask the hotel for a lower floor for you? Those old buildings have steep stairs and David mentioned you get winded."

"Simone. I'm okay."

"Totally! I only want you to feel looked after, Jenny."

I set the phone face down and dialed Linda.

Linda spent thirty-one years as a nurse, the last ten on a surgical floor, where she saw every day what swelling does when nothing drains it. Her husband walked out two years ago, when she was 58, and back then she was heavier than me. I lost track of her after that. Then last spring I ran into her at a cousin's wedding and didn't recognize her until she laughed.

Same Linda. Not the worn-out version I'd seen last. The one from before.

I read her the texts. The extra weight. The booths. The lower floor.

"That's David talking through her," Linda said.

"Believe me, I know."

"And he's not describing a woman. He's describing a backup. Your drain shut off two years ago and nobody fixed it. You didn't let yourself go, Jenny."

"I know."

Neither of us said anything for a second.

"Jenny. At that wedding you told me I looked different and I said I'd been walking. That wasn't all of it. I started something right after the divorce. I'm sending it to you now."

My phone buzzed with a link.

Lymphoria. Four herbs. Liquid drops.

"What is it."

"It's for the thing you keep calling weight. You don't have menopause weight. You have menopause sludge. When estrogen drops your lymphatic system stops working. Every glass of wine. Every hormone. Every toxin from the last thirty years that your body was supposed to drain. Just sits there. Locked in. Hardened. Stuck. That's not fat. That's waste. You can diet for the rest of your life. You'll never burn it off. Because you can't burn waste. You have to drain it."

"And drops do that."

"Four herbs do that. Stillingia breaks down the years of hardened waste. Prickly ash restarts the circulation menopause shut down. Cleavers sweeps the loosened waste out. Red clover calms the swelling so it doesn't build back up. I watched drainage change post-op patients for a decade. Nobody ever told the women on my floor it applied to them."

"Then why has no doctor said a word to me."

"Because nobody gets rich telling you you're backed up. They get rich selling you a weight-loss shot and a diet sheet. So women our age burn three years on salads and spin class wondering what we did wrong."

"Linda. I've got fourteen months."

"Plenty," she told me. "I started with less."

The bottle came four days later. Dark glass, a dropper, honey colored.

Half a dropper under my tongue every morning and every night. It tasted like honey.

Week one, I was in the bathroom every hour. By day five my face wasn't puffy when I woke up. I stood at the sink touching my cheekbones like they belonged to somebody else.

Week three, my belt went in a notch.

Week six, I zipped the good jeans from the back of the closet. Sat down in them. Stood up. Still zipped.

Week ten, I caught my profile in a shop window and my jawline was there. An actual line.

Month four, my wedding ring slid past the knuckle. I'd taken it off the night David left. I only wanted to know if it would go on. It did. I put it back in the dish.

Month five, the scale said nineteen pounds down. I hadn't changed what I ate.

Month seven, my hairdresser tilted my chin up in the chair and asked what I'd been doing, because she could see my neck again.

Month nine, I went shopping for Nadia's birthday dinner and came home with a black dress that hugged me. In the fitting room I couldn't stop looking.

Not 53. Not 43 either.

Just Jenny. The one who grew that department to sixty people. The one who raised two kids in a house where Dad was mostly a suitcase. The Jenny David saw every day for thirty years and then decided was gone.

Month twelve, Nadia came over on a Sunday to eat. She perched at the counter, watched me cook, and was quiet for ages.

Finally: "Mom. You're you again."

I didn't ask what she meant.

Neither of us needed it explained.

David and Simone had beaten me to the terminal and were standing in line. Simone had a big cream hard-shell suitcase, a matching tote, a garment bag over her arm. Every piece coordinated.

She turned and saw me.

She held the look a beat too long.

"Jenny. You look... amazing."

"Thank you, Simone."

"Have you been... doing something?"

"No," I said. "I slept well."

At the counter behind her, David was asking the agent how a suitcase could possibly be overweight.

I went to the bag drop.

The second night, Nadia put me opposite her friend Priya, who practices internal medicine.

Ten minutes into talking, she stopped halfway through a sentence.

"Jenny, sorry, I need to ask. What are you doing?"

"About what?"

"About whatever it is. Your face. Your jaw. You look like you've slept for a week. Nadia said the last couple of years were rough on you. They don't show. You look like someone who's been eating clean and training for a year straight."

I told her about Lymphoria.

She wrote it on a napkin.

"Stillingia. Cleavers," she read back. "Lymphatic."

"Four herbs. Half a dropper twice a day."

"Makes sense." She underlined it. "I see menopausal women every week who swell up and get handed a calorie chart. Nobody talks to them about drainage. Somebody should."

She glanced down the table.

Four seats away, Simone had her eyes on us, and had for a couple of minutes.

Priya folded the napkin into her purse.

Across the candles Nadia caught my eye. She didn't say anything. It was a smile I hadn't gotten from her in two years.

On day three we covered seven miles of the Gothic Quarter on foot.

Simone had to stop and sit twice.

I never did.

David never once spoke to me directly in Barcelona. He'd look over now and then like a man trying to place a face.

On the way home, Simone came and found me by the gate.

She took the seat next to mine. For a minute neither of us spoke.

"David's suitcase was over the limit again on the way back. Another $85."

"Too bad."

She turned to me.

"Priya gave me that napkin before she left."

"Did she."

"Lymphoria."

"Yes."

"David said you'd had a hard few years. That menopause changed you. That I needed patience about the weight, and how much you'd... slowed down."

I let that sit.

"That's not who's next to me."

"No," I said. "It's not."

Quiet.

"How long have you been taking it?"

"Fourteen months."

I watched her count backward.

"So you started the week Nadia told you about us."

"That week."

She looked down at her hands.

"I thought I was being nice. The booths. The heat text. The lower floor."

"I know."

"I was just repeating David."

"Yes."

"He doesn't actually know you."

"He did once."

"And you let me tell your daughter about a woman who isn't here anymore."

"For about a week. After that I stopped letting you. It just took you fourteen months to notice."

She nodded, slow.

"That's our group."

I grabbed my coat.

"Simone."

Her eyes came up.

"It's easy to order. It's real. I felt it the first week and I felt like myself by month four."

"How far ahead of me are you?"

"Fourteen months."

"Right."

I stood.

"Enjoy the flight home, Simone."

So here's what I know now.

David watched me run our life for thirty years. Then menopause hit and my belly swelled and my face puffed and my clothes stopped closing, and he made that the whole story. Then he left and passed it to somebody younger.

She sent me texts about the heat and my weight. About booths. About stairs.

She came to my daughter's thirtieth with a coordinated set of suitcases and the calm of someone who'd been assured she was the one keeping up now.

I came zipped into black, with my jaw back and my ring loose, fourteen months after a nurse told me I wasn't fat, I was backed up.

She never saw me coming. I'd seen her coming for over a year.

And I'd used every month of it.

Lymphoria didn't make me strong. I was strong the whole time.

It drained off the version of me David told everyone was permanent.

Simone watched a doctor copy the name onto a napkin. She watched me cover seven miles while she sat down twice. She saw the way my daughter looked at me, the way a girl looks when her mother finally comes home.

And at the gate she asked me how long.

By then I'd had fourteen months on her.

If somebody has given your daughter, your friends, his new wife a picture of you that you never signed off on.

If they've been planning around the woman in his description.

It was never your fault. It's your drain. And a drain can be opened.

Lymphoria. Stillingia, prickly ash, cleavers, red clover. Half a dropper under the tongue, morning and night. Tastes like honey.

They make it in small batches and it sells out. Linda waited five weeks on a restock once. If the button below still works, it's in stock right now.

30-day money-back guarantee. Nothing changes, you don't pay.

I felt it in a week. I felt like me in four months.

Simone's going to need that long too. She's on day one.

I started fourteen months before her.

Your move.
