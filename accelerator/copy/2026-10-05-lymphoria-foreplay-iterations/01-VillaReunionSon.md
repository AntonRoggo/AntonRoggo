## HOLD

Her name is Brooke. Thirty-nine. Married to my ex for fourteen months now.

Before I go any further, I want to say this plainly. This isn't me getting even. It's me finally refusing to be gracious about one phone call she made to my mother in July, and at sixty-two, I've earned that.

I'm Claire. Sixty-two. My son Ethan is seventeen. The divorce went through fourteen months ago. I manage a healthcare administration department, thirty-eight people report to me, and I came out of the settlement with a house in my own name. I'm the parent who drove Ethan to eight college campuses this spring. I'm the parent who sat beside him in the guidance office last November after a teacher mentioned he'd gone quiet and quit putting his hand up.

Greg gets him two weekends a month. He skipped Ethan's last two debate tournaments. He hasn't shown up to a single parent-teacher night since we split.

But Brooke volunteered to book everyone's flights for the reunion in Provence.

And now my mother thinks Brooke is the considerate one.

Mom has organized the reunion each August for fifteen years now. This summer she'd rented a villa near Aix-en-Provence. Five days. Everybody under one roof.

Five weeks out, she called me.

I knew the tone the second she spoke. Slow and padded, the way she sounds when she's bracing for me to be upset.

"Honey, I wanted you to hear it from me first. Greg's bringing Brooke."

I was standing at my kitchen counter. I had to pull out a stool.

"Mom. Greg and I are divorced."

"I know that, honey. But Ethan's used to having his father around. And Brooke's been lovely about all of it. She's taking care of the flights. Honestly, it was her suggestion."

"And you agreed."

"What was I going to tell her? She was being so sweet. I didn't want things to get uncomfortable."

I let the silence run.

"Let me think about it."

I ended the call.

Three days after that, Brooke rang me herself.

It had been more than half a year since we'd said a word to each other.

"Claire, hi! How are you doing? So, I was just on with Joan. Okay, so Greg and I have been having some conversations. A lot of them, honestly. About Ethan. With how much has been on you since the split, the long nights, the homework, all that campus touring, and Greg says you've really stopped taking care of yourself, we just feel like maybe it's kinder for everybody if we take more off your hands. Permission slips. The college trips. Some of the everyday stuff. Greg worries about you doing it by yourself. We only want what's right for Ethan."

"So you're phoning to let me know that you and Greg have decided, between yourselves, that I can't raise my own child."

"Claire, come on, there's no need to turn this into..."

"Brooke."

She went silent.

I hung up and dialed Denise.

Denise and I were roommates in college. These days she has her own consulting business, and she has never once softened a thing for my benefit.

I gave her all of it. Mom's call. Brooke's call. The part where she said "Greg and I have been having some conversations."

There was a pause on the line.

"Hold on. She said they're going to do what? Her and Greg? With YOUR kid?"

"Word for word."

I heard her exhale.

"Okay. Here's the deal. You don't walk into that villa ready for a fight. You walk in as the woman who's raised that boy from his first breath. The kind of woman people notice before she even says hello."

"And how exactly do I pull that off?"

"It starts with how you feel walking in. Which includes your body, Claire. Are you still at war with the scale every time there's something big coming up?"

She knew why that question would land.

My weight had turned into a wound well before the papers were signed.

The year I hit sixty, Greg stood in the bedroom doorway while I struggled with the zipper on a dress for a dinner party and said, "Didn't that thing used to fit you? It's all going to your face now."

He chuckled when he said it. As if he were doing me a favor.

After he left the room, I stood in front of the mirror. My belly was pushing against the zipper. My face was puffy, the way it was every morning by then. My jawline had melted down into my neck somewhere I hadn't noticed.

I took the dress off and hung it at the back of the closet.

And after that night, I never really worked out what to do about it.

I knew my body had changed. Menopause had started in my fifties and I'd held the line for years, and then somewhere around fifty-nine everything I'd been holding off seemed to arrive in one season. I was eating the way I'd always eaten. It just didn't matter anymore. And every fix I tried seemed to need another fix.

I cut carbs. Then sugar. Then I stopped eating until noon. Protein shakes in place of lunch. A walking group at six in the morning, three days a week. A fat burner from the drugstore that made my heart race. And shapewear, for the days when none of it had worked yet.

I watched the videos too.

Lean women, twenty-five, thirty-two, standing in spotless kitchens, explaining fasting windows and macros and how simple it all was once you "got into the rhythm."

I really tried.

I'd stop the video, go back, write down what she ate and when, then weigh chicken breasts on a little kitchen scale and copy her. Some weeks I'd get on the real scale on Sunday and be heavier than the Sunday before.

So, slowly, I quit trying so hard.

Not because I didn't care anymore.

Because I was worn out from doing everything right and still feeling like I'd failed.

Denise had watched all of it happen.

"Claire," she said. "Here's what Brooke will do. She'll arrive thirty-nine years old, with the waist to match and a closet full of sundresses. The story she's been feeding your mother is that you're tired and falling apart. Don't try to out-thirty-nine her."

"I'm not planning to."

"Good. Show up sixty-two and entirely yourself. Nobody can compete with that."

Then she told me about Lymphoria.

Her older sister Pam was an ICU nurse for thirty-one years. Last winter Pam's doctor told her the weight was "just menopause" and to eat less. Pam went digging on her own instead, and she's been on these drops since January.

"Drops?"

"Drops. And before you tell me that's ridiculous, I said the exact same thing to Pam."

Then Denise said she'd tell me what Pam told her, word for word, because Pam explained it better than she could.

"You don't have menopause weight. You have menopause sludge. When estrogen drops your lymphatic system stops working. Every glass of wine. Every hormone. Every toxin from the last thirty years that your body was supposed to drain. Just sits there. Locked in. Hardened. Stuck. That's not fat. That's waste. You can diet for the rest of your life. You'll never burn it off. Because you can't burn waste. You have to drain it."

I didn't say anything.

"Four herbs," Denise went on. "Stillingia breaks down the years of hardened waste. Prickly ash restarts the circulation menopause shut down. Cleavers sweeps the loosened waste out. Red clover calms the swelling so it doesn't build back up. Half a dropper under the tongue, morning and night. And here's the strange part. It tastes like honey."

"Honey?"

"Trust me. Pam's face is a different shape than it was at Christmas. Her jawline came back. She's sixty-six and she's wearing a belt again. I watched it happen across three Sunday dinners."

That was what got me.

I had no interest in looking younger than Brooke.

I had no interest in looking starved.

I just wanted to step out of the car at that villa looking like the woman who'd been running her life and raising her boy all along.

Denise texted me the link.

"Order from them directly. The cheap knockoffs online are weak doses and fillers. They make it in small batches, and there's a thirty-day money-back guarantee, which means you can take it for real before you decide anything."

I ordered that night.

The bottle came that Wednesday. The flight was four and a half weeks away.

I carried it upstairs and set it on the counter next to everything I'd been using. The kitchen scale. Two tubs of shake powder. The fat burner. The calorie app on my phone. The shapewear folded in the top drawer.

Then I unscrewed the dropper and put half of it under my tongue.

Honey. Denise was right.

Nothing else happened that night. I didn't expect it to.

The first week, I was in the bathroom every hour. I joked to Ethan that I'd turned into a houseplant.

On day four, I looked in the bathroom mirror before work.

Mornings were always the worst. The face I woke up to looked like someone had inflated it while I slept, and it took until lunch to come down.

That morning, it was still my face. There was just less of it.

I stood there a while.

By week two my belt went in a notch, and my mother's old ring, the one I wear on my right hand, started turning on my finger.

By week three I could see where my jaw stopped and my neck started. I hadn't been able to see that in two years.

At week four the scale moved, for the first time since I'd started weighing chicken.

The night before the flight I pulled the dress out of the back of the closet. The one from Greg's dinner party. It zipped. No shapewear underneath.

Then I went down the hall and sat on the end of Ethan's bed while he packed.

He glanced up.

"Mom. You look different."

For half a second my stomach clenched.

Then he grinned.

"Like, good different."

I laughed.

"I changed what I'm taking."

He gave me a look like that couldn't be the whole explanation.

But it was the whole explanation.

We got to the airport early on Friday.

Greg, Brooke, Mom and my stepfather were already in line at bag drop. Brooke had a set of three designer suitcases, all matching.

She spotted me first.

Her eyes went to my face. Then my waist.

Then to Ethan, beside me with his backpack on one shoulder.

Her smile shifted.

Went sweeter.

Which was worse.

"Oh, Claire. Honey. Look at you. You look... really good. Have you done something?"

I didn't answer her.

"Sweetie, are you sure you're up for this? Five days is a long time."

Ethan looked up at me.

"I'll be fine."

"Ma'am?" Brooke's agent tried again. "Your first bag's over the weight limit. That's a hundred dollars."

Brooke turned back to the counter.

Her smile slipped.

I took Ethan to the next counter over.

The agent handed us two boarding passes.

"You're all set. Enjoy your trip."

Ethan hooked his arm through mine and we headed for security.

Behind us, Brooke was hauling things from one suitcase into another at the desk while Greg just stood there.

Security took us twenty minutes. We sat down at the gate with forty minutes to spare.

A little while later the woman across the aisle lowered her phone. Mid-sixties. A silk scarf knotted at her throat.

"Forgive me. This is going to sound odd. You have the most beautiful jawline, and you look so rested. Have you lost weight? What are you doing?"

I almost laughed.

"Drops, believe it or not."

She sat forward.

"Drops?"

"Lymphoria."

Her eyes went back to my jaw, then down to her own lap.

"I've been fighting this belly for five years. My doctor wanted to put me on one of those weight-loss shots and I was sick for a month. You look like you've just come back from a spa week."

I pulled the bottle out of my purse.

"Four herbs. Half a dropper, morning and night."

She squinted at the tiny bottle.

"That's it?"

"So far."

She wrote the name on the back of her boarding pass.

We touched down in Marseille on Saturday morning.

The house sat thirty minutes outside Aix. Olive groves. Lavender. A long white gravel drive.

My cousin Holly was out in the courtyard.

Holly has a parenting account online. Somewhere around three hundred eighty thousand followers. She's been posting about Ethan and me at every birthday and graduation for years.

She watched me climb out of the car.

"Wait. Claire. Wait a second."

"What?"

"Your face. What happened to you?"

I laughed.

"Holly, that came out awful."

"You know what I mean. You look amazing. Your whole face is different."

I told her about the drops.

She already had her phone up.

"Stand right there. This light is perfect. My followers are going to lose it."

I let her take it.

Brooke's car was just rolling up the drive.

Two suitcases.

The airline had lost the third.

Saturday dinner was out on the back terrace. A long table, candles in glass, citronella burning, the whole family.

I wore the wrap dress. Half a dropper before I came down, same as every night at home.

Brooke appeared in a fitted black sundress and took the chair beside Greg.

I sat with my aunt Ruth.

Under the table, Ruth squeezed my hand.

"Ethan is the spitting image of you lately. And you, my dear, look better than at Christmas. What's different?"

"Mostly, I quit dieting."

She leaned in.

"Well, don't you dare start again."

My uncle leaned across the table.

"Claire, how's it all going? Joan tells me you've been handling everything on your own. That kid's lucky to have you."

At the far end of the table, Holly was already passing her phone to her sister to show her the courtyard photo.

Brooke was watching the whole table turn toward me.

She tried to get Greg to look at her.

He was busy with his plate.

Sunday morning I took my coffee out to the terrace. Ethan was down by the water with a book.

Brooke came out with a mug of her own and sat down beside me.

"Claire. I'd really like it if we could talk. While we're all here together."

I kept my eyes on the olive trees.

"There's nothing to discuss, Brooke."

"I know that call a few weeks back was hard to hear..."

"My son is seventeen. He already has a mother. My name's on his birth certificate. You've seen it. It's the name you wrote on the envelope of the card you sent him last December."

She had nothing to say to that.

I stood up and went inside.

That afternoon everyone was in the water.

For a couple of hours Brooke had been circling Ethan, calling him "buddy" a little too hard every time.

Ethan climbed out of the water and wrapped himself in a towel.

He walked past Brooke.

Came right over to my lounge chair.

"Mom, come down to the grove with me?"

Loud enough that the entire terrace heard.

Mom.

Not the cautious way he says it when Greg and I are both in the room.

The way that means me.

Brooke was three feet from him, a towel in her hands she'd meant to give him.

I slipped my arm through his.

The two of us headed down the hill toward the olive trees.

Every eye on that terrace followed us the whole way.

On Monday morning Holly posted the courtyard picture.

By lunch the post was past two hundred thousand views, with thousands of comments, all asking more or less the same thing.

What is she doing?

That jawline. How?

She looks ten years younger than her last photo.

Please tell us what she's taking.

Holly showed me her screen.

"Look at this."

The comments kept coming in while we watched.

Holly had pinned a reply under the photo.

Lymphoria.

Mom was reading over Holly's shoulder. A couple of cousins had texted asking for the link.

On a chaise across the terrace, Brooke was refreshing the post over and over.

I watched her refresh it for a solid ten minutes.

She didn't speak to me again all afternoon.

At dinnertime I was ready in under ten minutes.

Brooke hadn't come down yet.

Greg stood near the terrace doors, a glass of red in his hand.

He looked at me.

Then looked a second time.

"What did you do?"

"I changed what I take."

"Yeah, but what?"

I pulled the little bottle out of my purse.

"This."

He stared at it.

"That's all?"

"Morning and night."

He looked back up at my face.

"You look the way you used to."

I held his eyes.

"No, Greg. I look the way I look now."

He didn't answer.

A minute later Brooke came down the stairs.

She looked at him, then at me.

Neither of us said a thing.

Tuesday morning everyone was packing for the flight home.

Ethan's things and mine went into the rental. One trip.

Out in the courtyard, Brooke was trying to wedge three suitcases into their trunk. The lost one had finally shown up from Marseille on Monday.

Greg was inside on a call.

Mom came over to me at the car.

"Claire. Those drops of yours. Holly showed me all the comments last night."

She stopped.

Tried again.

"Where do you get them?"

I looked at her.

"Lymphoria. Order straight from them."

"That little bottle is all you've been doing?"

"Half a dropper. Morning and night."

She studied my face.

Then she gave one slow nod.

"I assumed you'd been starving yourself before this trip."

"Mom, I ate everything you put out all week."

That seemed to sit with her.

I touched her shoulder, briefly.

Then I got in.

Ethan had already claimed the front seat.

We pulled out before Brooke had the trunk closed.

Denise called the minute we landed. Ethan was asleep with his head on the window.

"Well?"

I went through all of it.

The overweight bag.

The scarf lady at the gate.

Holly's courtyard picture.

Aunt Ruth.

Ethan saying Mom in front of the whole terrace.

The views passing two hundred thousand.

"What about Brooke?"

"Spent five days watching."

Denise laughed.

Then she asked, "How many times did you reach for the shapewear?"

"Not once."

"Told you."

"You did."

So here's what I figured out.

Brooke never called about Ethan out of concern for how I was coping.

She phoned because she'd already made up her mind that I wasn't.

My own mother agreed when Brooke offered to come. Then she let it be dressed up as a favor.

I showed up anyway.

And by Sunday afternoon, Holly had my picture lined up for three hundred eighty thousand followers. Aunt Ruth had said my son was my spitting image. My boy, seventeen, had taken my arm in front of everyone and asked me down to the grove with him.

And the woman who'd tried to slide into his mother's place watched every minute of it for five days.

Lymphoria didn't do battle with Brooke on my behalf.

What it did was make me impossible to write off as the worn-out woman she'd already decided I was.

I spent years believing menopause meant I had to diet harder.

Less food when the scale went up. More walking when less food didn't work. Shakes when I was too tired to cook. Shapewear when nothing else showed.

I kept cutting, and kept wondering why my face got puffier.

It turned out what was sitting in me was never fat. It was waste menopause had locked in, and you can't diet your way out of waste. You have to drain it.

Stillingia breaks down the years of hardened waste. Prickly ash restarts the circulation menopause shut down. Cleavers sweeps the loosened waste out. Red clover calms the swelling so it doesn't build back up. Half a dropper under the tongue, morning and night. Tastes like honey.

It's third-party tested. No fillers.

But that isn't why I ordered a second bottle.

I ordered a second bottle because it takes ten seconds twice a day, and I like my body more now than I did after two years of diets.

They make it in small batches, and they stand behind it with a thirty-day money-back guarantee. You can take it for real before you decide.

Take it at home.

Take it on the plane.

Take it through the whole trip.

Take it right up to the family photo.

Then decide.

If the man you divorced married a woman who's decided she'd make a better mother to your child than you do, you already know exactly who I mean.

If your family quietly made space at the table for your replacement.

If you're the one who never stopped showing up while somebody else tried to slide into your seat.

You don't have to out-diet her.

You don't have to look younger.

You don't have to start one more diet to prove a single thing.

You just have to walk in looking completely, unmistakably yourself.

Brooke wanted to be Ethan's mother.

Five days in, she got to watch him pass her by, hook his arm through mine, and say "Mom."

She'd sold my family on the picture of an exhausted woman barely managing.

By Monday, two hundred thousand strangers were staring at a photo of that same woman, begging to know what she was taking.

The drops didn't win it for me.

Ethan won it.

But stepping into that villa with my boy on my arm and a body that felt like my own again, while Brooke spent five days watching from across the terrace?

That was never about the weight.

That was my answer to every person who'd decided I wasn't doing enough.
