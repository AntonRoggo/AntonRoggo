## HOLD

Richard and I had thirty-five years together. College sweethearts. Married at twenty-eight. We made a home in Portland and raised two kids there, both long since moved out. And for the better part of it, we really were happy.

Roughly three years ago, the seams started to give. Small stuff first. Then the bigger things. A counselor, for a while. It didn't help. By the time the papers were filed, both of us knew.

It was final eleven months ago. Half a year after that, Richard remarried. Amber. Forty-five. I'm sixty-four. Truthfully, it didn't bother me. By that point I was only glad to be done.

Living alone took getting used to. I won't pretend otherwise. Three and a half decades of dinner for two, and then it's a single plate at the counter. A bed with too much room on one side. Still, I found my way.

I repainted the kitchen in the sunny yellow he'd vetoed for years. I took up walking by the river at first light, while the rest of the neighborhood slept. The house was quiet again, and for once the quiet was mine.

Then the talk got back to me. From couples we'd been friends with since our thirties. Richard hadn't simply left. He'd been out there making me into a sad case.

How I'd crumbled the day he packed his car. How I'd stopped bothering with myself altogether.

And the part he apparently loved most: I'd ballooned. My face had puffed up and my jaw was gone, like I'd been eating straight from the fridge since he moved out. By the end, he claimed, he couldn't sit through dinner across from me. That, supposedly, was why he went.

He delivered it like a comedy routine. Thirty-five years, reduced to a punchline.

It was Jessica who finally told me, over coffee. She kept her eyes on the table. "Honey, people are repeating it to me now. To my actual face." She spun her cup slowly and never took a sip. "I tell them it's ridiculous. I just wanted you to get it from someone who loves you, not secondhand."

I was doing well. Better than well. But he'd spent close to a year casting me as the woman who collapsed the moment he quit holding her up.

Then an envelope came. Tom and Sarah, friends of ours for thirty years, were getting married in Charleston, six weeks out. Three days of festivities.

There on the guest list: my name. And Richard's.

My stomach sank. I hadn't seen him once since the signing. Now I'd get a whole weekend of him and Amber, surrounded by everyone we'd ever had over for dinner. Every person he'd told I'd ballooned.

I went back and forth for three days. Skipping it was the sensible choice. Send a nice gift, a polite note, let it all happen without me. Plenty of them likely assumed that's what I'd do. But not going felt like signing off on his whole version.

So I phoned my sister Maya, because she'd understand.

Maya's been a nurse for three decades, most of that on a geriatric unit. Hardly anything slips past her, and nothing rattles her. She went through her own divorce six years back. Her ex found someone much younger a little while later.

She knew the whole terrain. Mutual friends picking sides. The new wife. Rooms full of people watching to see if you'd fall apart. If anybody could walk me into that wedding, it was her.

I unloaded everything in one breath. The envelope. Richard. Amber. Three days with everyone we knew. And my body. The things he'd been saying about it. The things Jessica could barely say back to me.

Maya was silent a long moment. "Don't move. I'm driving over."

She's forty minutes away. She was knocking within the hour, a small canvas tote over her arm. She filled my kettle like she owned the place and pointed me to a chair.

"I'm going to tell you something, and you'll want to throw me out."

"Go on."

"All this weight talk of his." She looked me dead in the eye. "I've noticed it too. Your face puffier in the mornings. Your middle. For a while now."

I stayed quiet.

"So you think he's right about me."

"No. I think he's a liar. Your body changed, yes. He's lying about why, and he's lying about whose fault it is."

"Maya, I walk every single morning. I eat like a bird. I gave up wine at Easter."

"I know, sweetheart. This isn't about effort. I've spent thirty years nursing women our age, and I've seen this in more of them than you'd guess, and not one doctor ever sat them down and explained it." She laid her palm flat on the table. "You don't have stress weight. You have stress sludge. When cortisol stays high your lymphatic system stops working. Every glass of wine. Every hormone. Every toxin from the last thirty years that your body was supposed to drain. Just sits there. Locked in. Hardened. Stuck. That's not fat. That's waste. You can diet for the rest of your life. You'll never burn it off. Because you can't burn waste. You have to drain it."

"Cortisol."

"Three years of that marriage coming apart. Counseling. Lawyers. Him moving out and her moving in. Your cortisol's been sky high since before you ever filed. You didn't do this to yourself, Nicole. He did this to you."

I pressed my eyes shut. "And I was supposed to figure that out how?"

"You couldn't have, honey. You look at yourself every day. It happens too gradually to notice. A woman can carry a swollen face and a belly that won't budge for years and just chalk it up to getting older. And it's not on you. It's not laziness. It's not letting yourself go. It's what living with that man's stress did inside you. I've cared for hundreds of women with exactly this, and every one of them deserved better than a punchline."

"Then what do I do?"

Maya reached into the tote and put a little dark dropper bottle between us. "This. Lymphoria. Four herbs. Stillingia breaks down the years of hardened waste. Prickly ash restarts the circulation the stress shut down. Cleavers sweeps the loosened waste out. Red clover calms the swelling so it doesn't build back up. Half a dropper under the tongue, morning and night. Tastes like honey."

"Does it actually work?"

"Half the nurses on my unit take it. I've been on it since my own divorce." She stopped for a second. "One morning I noticed I wasn't turning sideways in the mirror anymore. I'd never even realized I did that."

I stared at the bottle and left it where it was. "If I take that, it's like saying he had a point."

"He was cruel. Cruel isn't the same thing as correct." She closed my hand over it. "Chin up and a dropper under your tongue. You can do both."

I kept the bottle.

"And get more ordered," she said, shrugging into her coat. "It's made in small batches. Once they're out, you wait weeks."

She pressed a kiss to my hair and headed home.

I tried it before bed. I didn't expect a thing. It tasted like honey. That was it.

But in the morning I caught myself lingering in front of the bathroom glass, for longer than I had all year. Not fixing anything. Only standing there, shoulders back, the way I used to stand.

That first week I was running to the bathroom every hour. On day four the morning puffiness had eased. By week two my belt had moved a notch, and my rings had begun to spin. By week four my jaw was visible again. The scale went down for the first time since the divorce.

Not the wreck Richard had been advertising. Just myself, back.

The night before my flight I laid everything out. Rehearsal dinner dress. Ceremony dress. Brunch dress. Three dresses I hadn't been able to zip in two years. Shoes. Earrings.

Then my toiletry bag. For the first time in ages, just one little bottle. No shapewear. No "forgiving" wrap dress packed as a backup. No cardigan to drape over my stomach for pictures. Years of pre-party panic, boiled down to one bottle.

Richard texted that morning: "Fly safe. And please don't turn this weekend into the Nicole show."

I left it on read.

Friday I touched down in Charleston.

That evening was the rehearsal dinner, at a restaurant over the harbor. I wore navy. Hair and makeup done properly.

The second I came through the door, I could tell. They'd all heard his story. You could read it on every face. Friends of twenty years flashed me a tight smile and turned away. No one came over. A couple of them looked, then very deliberately didn't.

I sat toward the far end. Both seats beside me stayed empty. He'd reached them before I had. They'd already decided.

Up near the head of the table, Richard and Amber were entertaining everyone. Amber had on a clingy satin dress, and the shapewear had rolled at her waist so the line showed straight through. Every few minutes she'd get up, yank the hem, and hold her breath for somebody's phone.

I considered heading up to my room. Slipping away before the speeches. Leaving them their story. I'd actually picked up my purse.

Then something in me said no. He doesn't get to edit me out of my own friendships. Not one more day.

I straightened in my chair and filled my wine glass.

Jessica reached me first. She bent for a hug, brief, the hesitant kind.

Then she stopped short. Pulled back to look at me properly. "Nicole. You look wonderful."

Her expression changed. She'd come over ready for the puffy, defeated woman Richard had described, and that woman wasn't in the chair.

A little later Sarah made her way down to me. "You look gorgeous tonight. Whatever you've been up to, keep it up."

"Thanks, Sarah."

"And your face. That jawline. You look ten years younger."

I smiled. "I feel it."

Across the room, Amber kept disappearing into the restroom. Each time she returned, she'd hauled the shapewear back up, and each time it had rolled down again by the next course.

The wedding was the following afternoon, out in a garden. I wore cream, the dress that hadn't fit since before the divorce. My half dropper that morning, and nothing under the dress holding anything in.

I arrived early and found my row. Richard and Amber slipped in a quarter of an hour late. In that heat, her dress was already bunching and pulling across her stomach where the shaper had quit.

Halfway through the vows, the woman beside me leaned in. "Sorry. You look amazing. What are you doing?"

"Honestly? Drops."

She sighed. "Four months on one of those weight-loss shots, and I still look swollen in every picture from this trip."

Cocktails that night were on the terrace.

Sarah's mother and I were chatting when Mark, Richard's brother, came over and wrapped me in a hug. "You look fantastic," he said. "Seriously, like a decade younger. What's your trick?"

"Drops. Lymphoria."

Richard stood just a few steps away, taking it in.

Mark faced him. "You said she'd let herself go. You said she'd ballooned. You said you couldn't stand sitting across the table from her."

I kept silent.

Mark looked at his brother a beat longer. Then at Amber, still pulling at her dress. "Looks like she's doing a lot better than you, Richard."

Sarah's mother chuckled softly. "She's had the whole room's attention all night."

Amber set down her drink, slowly. "You lied," she said under her breath, just for the two of us to hear. "About her. About everything."

"Amber, that's not..."

"I'm done with this conversation." She snatched up her clutch. "And I won't stand here beside you while the whole room tells your ex-wife how amazing she looks."

Richard's eyes went to Mark, then to me. "Sorry," he said, almost a whisper.

Then he went after her. We didn't see either of them again that night.

I stayed to the last song. Danced. Laughed till my face hurt. The best night of my whole year.

Sunday morning, a text from Maya: "Well??"

"Even better than I'd hoped."

"Told you. Go order another bottle right now. Small batches. They won't hold one for you."

I flew home that afternoon with one little bottle in my bag. It was all I'd needed.

Eleven months of Richard convincing everyone I'd come undone when he walked out. One weekend erased it. I didn't argue. I didn't explain myself. I simply showed up rested. Lighter. Younger than I'd looked in years. The woman he was a fool to leave.

So if someone's been spreading a story about you that keeps outrunning you, maybe winning the argument isn't the point. Maybe the point is draining what he left behind.

It's called Lymphoria. The button's below.

They make it in small batches, and there's a 30-day money-back guarantee. I wouldn't wait.

For a year Richard told anyone who'd listen that I'd let myself go. At the end of it, I didn't need to say anything. I just walked in.
