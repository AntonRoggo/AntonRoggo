### SCRIPT BODY

Richard and I were married for thirty-five years.
Two kids, both grown. Built a whole life together in Portland.
And then about three years ago, the marriage started coming apart.
By the time the divorce was final eleven months ago, we both knew it was over.
Six months later, Richard remarried.
Her name is Amber. Forty-five years old, eighteen years younger than me.
And honestly? I didn't even care. By then, I was just relieved to be out.

The first few months alone were strange, but I found my feet.
I painted the kitchen the yellow Richard always vetoed. I took my morning walks down by the river.
And then my friend Jessica met me for coffee.
And she could barely look me in the eye, and she said:
"Nicole... people are repeating things. To my face."
And I said:
"What are they saying?"
And she said:
"Richard has been going around telling everyone that you fell apart the day he walked out. That you stopped taking care of yourself, let the house go to ruin... and that toward the end, he couldn't even share a bed with you."

He'd turned thirty-five years of marriage into a punchline.
That night, I stood in front of the bathroom mirror for the first time in a year.
I'm sixty-three. I had gained thirty pounds during the last three miserable years of that marriage, and not an ounce had moved.
My belly was this hard, tight shelf pushing out against my waistband.
My face was puffy every morning. My jawline was completely gone.
I walked three miles every day. I cut wine. I cut bread. The scale hadn't moved a single pound.

And then the mail arrived.
An invitation to our friends Tom and Sarah's wedding down in Charleston. Seven weeks away.
Both our names were on the guest list.
Mine. And his.
He and his new young wife, in front of every single friend we'd shared for thirty years.
Every person he'd told I was a trainwreck.
For three days, I couldn't decide if I should even go.
Staying home felt sensible, but it felt like signing my name to every lie he'd told.

So I called my sister Maya.
Maya has been a registered nurse for thirty years, mostly on a geriatric ward.
She came straight over to my kitchen, put the kettle on, and she said:
"How are you really doing, Nicole?"
And I told her everything in a rush. The rumors. What Richard said. That Charleston wedding.
And then Maya looked right at me, and she said:
"I've got to say something, and you're going to want to throw me out."
And I said:
"What?"
And she said:
"That weight he keeps going on about? He's right that something is there. But he's a liar about what it is."
And I said:
"Maya, I walk every single morning! I barely eat!"
And then she said:
"This has got nothing to do with diet and exercise. Thirty years I've nursed women our age. When a woman spends three straight years holding a dying marriage together, cortisol stays through the roof.
And when cortisol stays that high, your lymphatic system completely shuts down.
That's your body's drain. It carries out all the dead cells, old hormones, and cellular waste your body makes every single day.
It has no internal pump.
And when it shuts down, nothing leaves.
Every night you couldn't sleep, every day you held that house together, it all just sat there.
It thickened. It hardened into sludge. In your belly, in your face, under your jaw.
That's not fat, honey. That's waste.
Think of a sink with a clogged drain: you can run less water, but it still fills.
You can starve all you want. You can walk all you want.
You cannot burn waste. You have to drain it.
He put that waste on you with three years of misery, and then had the nerve to tell everyone you let yourself go."

And I looked at her, and I said:
"Then how do I get rid of it?"
And she said:
"You open the drain."

And then she reached into her canvas bag, pulled out a small dark bottle, and set it on the table between us.
The label said Lymphoria.
And she said:
"Four botanical herbs. Stillingia breaks down the years of hardened waste sludge. Prickly ash restarts the circulation the stress shut down. Cleavers sweeps the loosened waste completely out. And Red clover calms the swelling so it doesn't build back up.
Half a dropper under your tongue morning and night.
Tastes sweet like honey.
Because it's liquid under the tongue, it enters your bloodstream in fifteen minutes.
I've sent half my hospital ward home with it, and I've used it myself since my divorce."
And then she paused, and she said:
"One morning it dawned on me I'd stopped bracing every time somebody hugged me. Never even knew I'd been doing it."

I looked at the bottle. I didn't pick it up.
And I said:
"Taking that feels like saying he was right about me."
And she closed my fingers around it, and she said:
"He was cruel about you. That isn't the same as right. You can hold your head up and take a dropper at the same time."
And then she said:
"They make it in small batches, so order more while you can.
You've got seven weeks until that wedding. Give it six."

I took it that night.
Week one: in the bathroom every hour. By day four, the puffiness in my morning face was gone. By day six, my rings were spinning loose.
Week two: belt one notch tighter. The hard, swollen belly started softening, like someone let the air out of a tire.
Week three: my jawline was back. Maya stopped by after her hospital shift, looked at me in the doorway, and said: "There she is."
Week four: down fourteen pounds of pure trapped waste and fluid. Work clothes draping easily.
Week five: the cream dress. A designer dress in the back of my closet I'd bought two years ago and could never zip.
I slid it on. It zipped right up.
Flat stomach. Defined waist. I stood there in it and cried happy tears.

The night before Charleston, I packed.
Three dresses. Not a single pair of shapewear. No suck-in underwear. No backup size.

That Friday, I walked into the rehearsal dinner on the water.
I came in my navy dress, hair done, shoulders back.
Further down the table, Richard and Amber were holding court.
And I could see Amber from across the room. Sucking in every time she stood. Tugging her dress down over her hips. Sitting straighter than anyone needs to.
Jessica came over, hugged me, pulled back with wide eyes, and she said:
"Nicole... you look incredible. You look completely at peace."

The next evening was the reception. Cocktails out on the terrace.
I wore that cream dress. Nothing underneath it but me.
And while I was talking with our friends, Richard's brother Mark walked over, gave me a huge hug, and he said:
"Nicole, you look unbelievable! What on earth have you been doing?"
Richard was standing six feet away, holding a martini, watching us.
And I looked at Mark, and I smiled.
And I didn't say a word.

And then Mark turned around, looked right at Richard, and in front of everyone, he said:
"You told me she'd let herself go. That you couldn't stand to be near her."
And Richard didn't say anything.
And Mark said:
"She's doing a lot better than you are, Richard."

Richard's face went completely dead.
Amber looked between Mark and Richard, her face burning with embarrassment.
She turned to Richard, hissed:
"You told me she was a trainwreck. You made a fool out of me."
And she grabbed her clutch and walked straight out to the valet.
Richard looked at Mark. Then he looked at me.
Muttered: "I'm sorry."
And he went after her.
They didn't come back for the rest of the night.

I stayed. I danced. I laughed with friends I'd had for thirty years.
Best night I'd had in a year.

Here's what I want you to know:
If there's a man, a divorce, or years of stress that made you feel like you lost your body...
If your stomach has that hard, swollen shelf that refuses to move no matter how little you eat...
You didn't let yourself go.
High stress shut your lymphatic drain, and years of cellular waste have been trapped with nowhere to go.
You can't starve away waste. You have to drain it.
