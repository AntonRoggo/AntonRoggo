## Phase 3: Execution

### HOOKS

**V1**
My husband's "work wife" came to Hawaii with us. Our anniversary. His idea. "Don't make this weird," he said.
I didn't make it weird.
I walked in on them on day three.
And six weeks later, I made sure he remembered exactly who he was losing.

**V2**
On the third morning of our thirty-fifth anniversary trip, I pushed open a hotel room door about four inches and saw my husband with the twenty-nine-year-old he'd invited along.
I didn't scream. I closed the door and went to the sunrise breakfast I'd booked in March. Alone.
What my best friend flew in to show me six weeks later is the only reason I didn't crumble.

**V3**
My husband invited his twenty-nine-year-old "work wife" on our thirty-fifth anniversary trip and told me not to make it weird.
She was in his room by day three.
I was in a navy dress six weeks later, and he couldn't look at anyone else.

### HOLD

Let me back up.

I was upstairs packing when Gary called from the driveway that he had a surprise. The surprise was standing at my front door. Chloe. Twenty-nine. Blonde highlights, glossy lips, and a little duffel over one shoulder like she was headed to brunch.

"She just went through a rough breakup, so she's coming with us. I knew you wouldn't mind. She can even help you get ready for the dinners."

She can help ME get ready for MY trip. Our thirty-fifth anniversary trip. The one I planned. The one I booked five months ago, the resort, the luau, the sunrise breakfast, the anniversary dinner by the water. The one I had been counting down to since January.

And he invited her.

I said, "Gary, this is our anniversary."

"Clair, come on. She's just a friend. Don't make this weird."

Chloe smiled at me. That smile. The one younger women give older wives when they think they've already won something.

"I really appreciate you being okay with this, Clair. Gary talks about you all the time at the office."

I bet he does.

I went upstairs and sat on the edge of the bed for ten minutes. Two dinner dresses laid out. A new swimsuit I had almost talked myself out of buying. My shapewear beside the mirror like surgical equipment. Two sizes of everything. The swimsuit I had bought two sizes up and told myself was "forgiving."

The things a woman packs when she is getting ready to spend her anniversary next to a twenty-nine-year-old her husband invited.

And then I got angry. Not the loud kind. The quiet kind, where something clicks behind your eyes and you think clearly for the first time in months.

I packed the swimsuit.

At the airport, Gary was at the counter arguing because one of his bags was overweight. Chloe was crouched beside her duffel, digging through three makeup bags because she could not remember where she had put her setting powder.

I walked past both of them.

Gary didn't look up.

Day one, Maui. The shuttle driver rearranged the back twice to fit their bags. I got on, opened my book, and pulled my cardigan closed over the swimsuit I never took off.

Day two was the luau. Chloe came downstairs in a cutout dress. I wore the coral one, the forgiving one.

Later, a photographer offered to take our picture. Gary stepped toward Chloe without thinking. Just one step. Small enough that most people would not have noticed.

I noticed.

The photographer looked between the three of us.

"Anniversary couple in the middle, please."

Gary moved back beside me. Chloe moved to the edge.

When the flash went off, I was smiling, because I already understood something. Gary had brought her because he wanted me to feel replaceable.

But standing there, he was the only one who looked unsure of where he belonged.

Day three. The sunrise breakfast I had booked back in March.

I woke up at five. Gary's side of the bed was cold. I figured he'd gone down early to get us a table by the water, because that's the man I'd been married to for thirty-five years.

Gary had booked our rooms next to each other, because of course he had.

I went next door to wake Chloe, because that's who I am.

The door was cracked. Not closed. Cracked.

I pushed it open about four inches. And I saw enough.

I didn't scream. I didn't cry. Not yet.

I closed the door. Walked downstairs. Sat at the sunrise breakfast I had booked in March, alone, my coffee in one hand, and watched the ocean turn gold behind an empty chair.

Then I went to the lobby and changed my flight.

Gary texted from the room: "Where are you??"

"A12. Been here a while."

I was staying with Sandra by the time they landed.

Sandra has been a flight attendant for sixteen years. Forty-three countries. Three marriages between the women on her crew, two divorces, and enough stories about husbands behaving badly to fill a book.

I told her everything. The driveway. The duffel. "She can even help you get ready." The door.

Sandra was quiet for five seconds.

"He said she could help you get ready. For YOUR anniversary trip."

"Yes."

She laughed once. Not a kind laugh.

The papers were drafted that week.

That night I stood in Sandra's guest bathroom and looked at myself for the first time in three years.

I'm 58.

Menopause hit me at 51 and never let up. Thirty pounds in three years. My belly never went down. My face was puffy every morning. My jawline was gone. My skin had lost its glow.

Standing in that mirror, I almost believed him. Maybe a twenty-nine-year-old was the only kind of woman a man like Gary could still see.

I'd tried everything for three years. I dieted. Cut sugar. Cut wine. Walked every morning. Pilates twice a week with women twenty years younger. Nothing moved.

My doctor didn't even look up. Said it's just menopause. Try to eat less. Walk more. I cried in my car.

Sandra had a Tokyo turn the next morning. She called from the crew lounge.

"I'm swapping out of it. I'm on the next flight home. Don't argue."

She walked in that night, dropped her flight bag, didn't hug me first. Put her hands on my shoulders and said, "We have work to do. You are not staying like this."

Three days later she set a small dark dropper bottle on the kitchen counter. The liquid inside looked like honey.

"Sixteen years of twelve-hour legs at altitude. Every long-haul crew I have ever flown with gets drained in São Paulo. It's the first thing the Brazilian girls teach you. You know the women on my crew who've been flying thirty years and still get carded at fifty-five? The grandmothers on Ipanema with skin like glass? They're not luckier than you. They're not more disciplined than you. They get drained. Every single day. It's the secret nobody tells you."

I looked at her like she was crazy.

"Sandra, I gained thirty pounds. I don't need drainage. I need to lose weight."

She looked me right in the eye.

"Nothing is wrong with you. You disappeared. Thirty-five years of that man and you disappeared. You don't have menopause weight. You have menopause sludge."

"When estrogen drops, your lymphatic system stops working. Every glass of wine. Every hormone. Every toxin from the last thirty years that your body was supposed to drain. It just sits there. Locked in. Hardened. Stuck. In your belly. In your face. Under your jaw. That's not fat. That's waste. You can diet for the rest of your life. You'll never burn it off. Because you can't burn waste. You have to drain it."

She picked up the bottle.

"Four herbs. Stillingia breaks down the years of hardened waste. Prickly ash restarts the circulation menopause shut down. Cleavers sweeps the loosened waste out. Red clover calms the inflammation so it doesn't build right back up. Half a dropper under your tongue in the morning. Half at night. Tastes like honey."

"Sandra, there's no way drops fix three years of this."

"Six weeks. By week one you'll be peeing constantly. By week three your waist will be smaller. By week six you won't recognize yourself. Watch."

Week one. Bathroom every hour. Day four, my face less puffy in the morning. Day six, my wedding ring spinning loose on my finger. I took it off and put it in the drawer.

Week two. Belt one notch tighter. The bloat that lived in my belly for three years. Gone.

Week three. Jawline back. Skin clearer than it had been in ten years. Sleeping through the night for the first time since 51. Sandra came in off a red-eye, stopped in the kitchen doorway, and said, "There she is."

Week four. Down fourteen pounds. Eating normally. Gary called twice. I let it ring.

Week five. Down nineteen pounds. The navy dress in the back of the closet. The one I bought for Hawaii and never wore. Fit me. I put it on with my grandmother's earrings and cried.

Week six. Sandra made a reservation at the restaurant on the water back home. The one Gary and I went to every anniversary for twenty years, before he decided Hawaii needed a chaperone.

"Why there?"

"Because you're not hiding from a restaurant."

I was at the bar fifteen minutes early. Navy dress. My grandmother's earrings.

A man about my age moved his jacket off the empty stool beside him and asked if I was waiting for someone.

"Apparently," I said.

He laughed.

Then I looked up, and Gary walked in.

With her.

The same twenty-nine-year-old. Wearing the blue shirt I bought him last Christmas. She was hanging on his arm.

They sat four tables away. He didn't see me.

Halfway through my drink I felt eyes on me.

I looked over, and my husband was staring. Mouth slightly open. Like he'd seen a ghost.

The man beside me followed my eyes. Then raised his glass toward Gary, friendly, the way men do.

"You're a lucky man."

Gary looked at me. Really looked at me.

"Yes," he said. "I am."

Too late.

I excused myself to the restroom.

A woman was at the sink next to mine. Late fifties. Beautiful but exhausted. She looked at me in the mirror.

"I'm sorry. I have to ask. You look incredible. You can't be over fifty. My husband just left me and I look like death."

I told her about Sandra. About the drops. About what the Brazilian crews know.

She pulled out her phone right at the sink and ordered.

I walked out, and he was standing there. Alone.

"Clair. You look..."

He didn't finish.

I didn't smile. I didn't speak. I walked past him.

Back at the bar, I was glowing. Not from the man with the jacket. From me.

Gary called two days later.

"I know you're angry. Can we talk?"

"My attorney will be in touch."

"Attorney? Clair, come on."

"The papers are already drafted, Gary. They were drafted the week I got home."

Silence. Then, quieter: "Please don't do this."

"You brought her on our anniversary."

I hung up. Then I called Sandra into the kitchen, because she had earned the whole story.

I told her about the man at the bar who called Gary lucky. The woman at the sink who ordered before she'd dried her hands. Gary standing outside the restroom with nothing to finish his sentence with.

And I told her Chloe had texted that morning.

"Clair. Can I ask you something. Those drops. Where did you get them."

Sandra was quiet for a second.

"What did you tell her?"

"Seventy percent off."

"You're kinder than I am."

Here's what I learned.

My husband brought a twenty-nine-year-old on our thirty-fifth anniversary and told me not to make it weird.

I didn't.

She arrived with glossy lips, three swimsuits, and the confidence of a woman who thought youth made her unforgettable.

I came home with a drafted set of papers and a best friend who knew what to do.

The drops didn't save my marriage. It was never going to.

They just made the truth impossible to miss. For me. For him. For her.

If your husband made you feel like you weren't enough.

If he compared you to someone younger without ever saying the words.

If you stopped buying the dress in your size because there wasn't one.

You need to try Lymphoria.

Not because drops can fix what someone else broke.

But because looking in the mirror and watching your own face come back, even, natural, and completely yours?

That is not about looking younger.

It is about looking like yourself again.

One small dark bottle. Four herbs. Stillingia, prickly ash, cleavers, red clover. Half a dropper under your tongue in the morning, half at night. Tastes like honey.

Sandra keeps one in her flight bag.

Chloe ordered two.

Gary is asking me not to sign.

I'm not asking anyone for anything.

Right now it's 70 percent off. 30-day money-back guarantee. Link below.

For the woman you are now, and the woman you are ready to see again.
