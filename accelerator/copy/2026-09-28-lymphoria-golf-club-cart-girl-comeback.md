## HOLD

Mark sold his software firm two years ago and retired.

That's when it started.

Golf five days a week. Long lunches at the clubhouse. His whole world became tee times and the four men in his foursome, most of them on their second wives. Women in their late thirties.

And Mark started noticing things out loud. Never about me. Just out loud, in the car.

"Dave's wife walked past our table today and every head in the dining room turned. Woman's got a waist like a college kid."

I told myself he was just making conversation.

Then the second wives started getting traded too. Dave showed up to the club Christmas party with a girl from the pro shop. Steve's new one was twenty-six and called him "babe" in front of everyone. And Mark started coming home different. New golf shirts, fitted. Whitened his teeth. Started going to the guys' trips in Scottsdale that he used to skip. His phone went face down on the counter. He laughed at texts and didn't say at what.

He stopped looking at me somewhere in there. Not all at once. The way a tide goes out.

Then one night in January he stopped at our bedroom doorway, arms crossed, and said, "Becca, you've gotten a little soft. I don't know how else to put it. Maybe look into something. For yourself."

For yourself.

Two weeks later, a Tuesday, I drove to the club to surprise him for lunch. He'd left his reading glasses on the counter. I parked by the cart barn because the lot was full.

He was in a cart behind the barn with the girl who drives the beverage cart. Kaylee. Twenty-two. His hand where it hadn't been on me in two years. She was laughing at something he said.

He's sixty-six.

I didn't scream. I didn't get out of the car. I put his glasses on the passenger seat, drove home, and sat at the kitchen table and stared at the wall until it got dark.

He came home at six and asked what was for dinner.

That night, after he fell asleep, I stood in the bathroom and looked in the mirror for the first time in two years.

I'm sixty-three.

Twenty-two pounds since I turned sixty. My lower stomach was swollen all the time. My eyelids were puffy every morning. My jawline had gone into my neck. The woman in the mirror looked like she'd been carrying wet laundry up three flights of stairs and never set the basket down.

I stood there and thought, I know what a twenty-two-year-old has that I don't.

I almost believed him.

Because I had tried. I walked four miles every morning for a year. I cut out bread. I quit the glass of wine with dinner. I logged every bite on the app my daughter set up for me. Nothing moved. My doctor looked at my chart for thirty seconds. "At sixty-three, hormones change. Eat less, walk more." I was already walking four miles. I cried in the car.

The next morning I called my sister.

Patrice lives in Salvador now. Twenty years in Brazil. Married a Brazilian man. Trained at a lymphatic clinic in Rio. I told her about the doorway. I told her about the cart barn. I told her about the mirror.

She said, "Sis, I'm on the next flight. Don't argue."

She got off the plane on Thursday. Didn't hug me first. Didn't cry with me. Put her hands on my shoulders and said, "We have work to do. You are not staying like this."

Three days into her visit she put a small dark dropper bottle on the kitchen counter. The liquid inside looked like honey.

She said, "You know those women who never seem to age? The ones who look forty-five at sixty-five? The Christie Brinkleys. The Jane Seymours. The women at your club on their third husband with a waist like a college kid. They're not luckier than you. They're not more disciplined than you. They get drained. Every single day. It's the secret nobody tells you."

I looked at her like she was crazy. "Patrice, I gained twenty-two pounds. I don't need drainage. I need to lose weight."

She looked me right in the eye. "You don't have sixty weight. You have menopause sludge. When estrogen drops, your lymphatic system stops working. Every glass of wine. Every hormone. Every toxin from the last thirty years that your body was supposed to drain just sits there. Locked in. Hardened. Stuck. In your belly. In your face. Under your jaw. That's not fat. That's waste. You can walk four miles for the rest of your life. You'll never burn it off. Because you can't burn waste. You have to drain it."

She picked up the bottle. "It's called Lymphoria. Four herbs. Stillingia breaks down the years of hardened waste. Prickly ash restarts the circulation menopause shut down. Cleavers sweeps the loosened waste out. Red clover calms the inflammation menopause caused. Half a dropper in the morning, half at night, under the tongue."

I said, "There's no way drops can fix three years of this."

She said, "Six weeks. By week two you'll be peeing constantly. By week three your waist will be smaller. By week six you won't recognize yourself. Watch."

I didn't tell Mark I knew. I moved into the guest room and told him my back hurt. He didn't argue. He was at the club by seven.

Week one. Bathroom every hour. Day four, my face less puffy in the morning. Day six, my wedding ring spinning loose on my finger. Told no one.

Week two. Jeans loose through the hips. The bloat that lived in my lower belly for two years, gone. Emma called on Sunday and said, "Mom, you sound different." I said I was sleeping better.

Week three. Jawline back. Skin like it hadn't been in ten years. Slept six hours straight for the first time since Mark sold the company.

Week four. Down fourteen. Eating normal meals. Mark said nothing. He'd spent two years not looking at me, and he wasn't going to start now. Patrice caught me in the hall mirror and said, "There she is."

Week five. Down nineteen. The dress I bought for his retirement party and never wore because it was a size too small. It fit. I put it back on the hanger and left it on the bedroom door where he'd see it.

He didn't.

Week six. The club's spring member dinner. Mark had told me in February I didn't have to come. "It's mostly the guys." I RSVP'd for two. Me and Patrice.

I walked into that dining room in the dress.

Every head turned.

Not Dave's wife. Me.

Mark was at the foursome's table by the window with his back to the door. Dave saw me first. Then Tom. Then Steve, who stopped mid-sentence and said, "Is that Becca?"

Mark turned around.

Mouth slightly open. Like he'd seen a ghost.

Kaylee was working the bar. She looked at me, then at Mark, then back at her glasses.

In the ladies' room, a woman at the next sink, about forty, one of the second wives, caught my eye in the mirror. "I'm sorry. I have to ask. You're Mark's wife? You look incredible. You can't be over fifty."

I said, "Sixty-three. My husband thinks I'm getting a little soft."

She laughed. I told her about the drops. She pulled out her phone right at the sink and ordered.

When I walked back out, Mark was standing by our table.

"Becca? You look incredible. What did you do?"

I didn't smile. I didn't answer. I put my hand on Patrice's arm and walked past him to the far side of the room, where Dave's wife had saved us two seats.

I looked back once. He was still standing there.

Emma came home two weeks later, saw the bottle on the counter, and said, "Mom, is this why you look like that?" She took it back to Austin. Two weeks after that she sent a photo. "I look like you."

Here's what I know now.

I thought he stopped looking at me because I got old. He didn't. He stopped looking because he was a coward. He couldn't handle a real woman his own age, so he went and found a girl who laughs at his jokes for tips. I let myself believe him for one night. Menopause just locked everything in. The second I drained it, I got me back. Not thirty-year-old me. The me I was supposed to be at sixty-three. Sharp. Awake. Alive. The woman he should've been terrified to lose.

I filed in April. He moved into a condo by the club. Kaylee quit in May. I hear Dave's wife told her to.

Look. I don't know where you're at right now. Maybe your husband retired and found a foursome and a doorway to stand in. Maybe you've walked four miles a day for a year and cut the bread and the wine. Maybe your doctor looked at your chart for thirty seconds.

Don't let them sell you Ozempic and hormone therapy when your body just needs to drain what menopause locked in.

Lymphoria. Four herbs. Half a dropper morning and night. Patrice said they sell out constantly. Small batches. I checked yesterday and they were almost out. If you still see the orange button below, you got lucky. Sixty-day money-back guarantee. But honestly, after six weeks, you won't need it.

That's all it took for me.
