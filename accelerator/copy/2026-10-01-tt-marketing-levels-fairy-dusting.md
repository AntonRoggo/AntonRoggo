# TT: "Our Industry Has Been Lying To You For Decades" (marketing levels / fairy dusting) (2026-10-01)
Reference: Alec Batis, Sweet Chemistry. Surgical: sentence order and wording kept; speaker identity and product section swapped. Voice: Ben, as a skincare founder, not a chemist.

Hey everybody, it's Ben, I'm the founder of The Tallowed Truth, and I've been in the skincare industry for [X years].
I'm here to talk to you about something that's very sensitive, that a lot of people in our industry do not want me to bring up.
The only reason why I can bring it up is because I don't answer to a corporate parent. I own this company.
What I'm referring to is the use of "marketing levels" in our industry, and how that relates to product quality and efficacy.
I'll start by letting you know that almost every cream on the shelf is formulated with what's called marketing levels, and it's been that way for decades.
Marketing levels are when a formulator or chemist adds about 0.01% or 0.001% of any given ingredient to a formula, for what they call label claim, or for story. So that marketing can speak about them, yet they really don't add any cost to the formula.
At the same time, many of the ingredients that brands formulate with come from suppliers who have done their own testing on these ingredients, to see what the optimum recommended usage level is.
Because those tests are done by the suppliers in-house, chemists and formulators usually take those results with a little grain of salt, yet they know there is some evidence that there is efficacy at those certain usage levels.
However, in order to bring the cost of a formula down, brands will often ignore those usage levels and put in 0.01% or 0.001% of that ingredient, just so that it appears on the ingredient list and marketing can speak about it.
The other reason why brands use marketing levels is because in most creams, or water-based emulsions, there's only about 10 to 15% room for functional active ingredients. Because there needs to be space for enough water and emulsifier in order to keep the cream stable.
What that means is, when marketing wants a lot of different claims in a product, oftentimes the chemist has to choose between which ingredients they'll put in for function, and which they're going to put in at marketing levels, for just story.
When I made F-Balm, I took the water out completely. Zero. So there's no emulsifier and no preservative taking up space, and everything in the tin is there to do a job. Grass-fed tallow, olive oil, honey, beeswax, and frankincense. Five ingredients, and that's the whole list.
For me, that's just giving people what they thought they were getting in the first place.
The challenge with that is that it's not a light, fluffy cream. It's a solid balm. You have to warm it between your fingers, and we have to make it ourselves, in small batches, in our own facility. So it is a challenge to formulate without water and without marketing levels, but it can be done, and I think it's time that we, the industry, make efforts to move in that direction.
So, we're here to invite all brands to take the pledge of using no marketing levels. No fairy dusting.
I feel like we should just evolve past what we used to do, and move into the future, and do right by our customer.
So the net net of taking the pledge to abolish all marketing levels in our industry will result not only in better product quality, but also the ability for our customers to have more respect and trust in our products, as well as our industry.
